An FIR can have serious consequences for the person named in it, but registering a case does not establish guilt. In appropriate circumstances, the High Court can quash an FIR and the criminal proceedings arising from it when continuing the case would amount to an abuse of the legal process or otherwise defeat the ends of justice.

FIR quashing is not automatic. The court examines the allegations, applicable law, supporting material and circumstances of the case. A claim that an FIR is false, a disagreement between parties, or even a settlement does not by itself guarantee that the proceedings will end.

This guide explains the meaning of FIR quashing, the recognised legal grounds, the procedure for approaching the High Court, the documents commonly needed, and the situations in which a quashing petition may not succeed.

---

## What Is FIR Quashing?

FIR quashing is a judicial remedy through which a High Court sets aside a First Information Report (FIR), or the criminal proceedings arising from it, when the legal requirements for exercising that power are satisfied.

An FIR is a formal record of information relating to an alleged cognizable offence. It initiates or records the beginning of the criminal-law process; it is not a finding that the accused committed the offence.

When an FIR does not legally disclose an offence, proceedings are barred by law, or the criminal process is being misused in circumstances recognised by precedent, an affected person may ask the jurisdictional High Court to intervene.

The important distinction is that the High Court does not quash every FIR that the accused disputes. It exercises an exceptional judicial power within established legal principles.

---

## Which Law Governs FIR Quashing in India?

The main statutory provision for inherent powers of the High Court is **Section 528 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS)**. It corresponds to **Section 482 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (CrPC)**.

Section 528 preserves the High Court's inherent powers to make orders necessary to give effect to orders under the BNSS, prevent abuse of the process of a court, or otherwise secure the ends of justice. The BNSS came into force on **1 July 2024**. Its repeal-and-savings provision must also be considered when determining which procedural law applies to a particular pending matter.

Older Supreme Court judgments often refer to Section 482 CrPC. The principles developed under that provision remain important when courts apply its successor, Section 528 BNSS, because the inherent-power language is substantially the same.

The precise provision and procedural route should nevertheless be checked against the facts, the procedural stage and the applicable transition rules. The date on which the FIR was registered is not a substitute for examining those issues.

## When Can a High Court Quash an FIR?

The Supreme Court's landmark decision in **State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal** and the principles reiterated in subsequent judgments provide an essential framework for evaluating whether criminal proceedings may be quashed.

The categories identified by the Supreme Court are illustrative, not an automatic checklist. The court considers the particular allegations and applicable law rather than granting relief merely because a petitioner labels a case false or malicious.

### 1. The FIR Does Not Disclose the Ingredients of an Offence
This may be an important ground where the allegations are accepted at face value but still fail to satisfy the essential legal elements of the offence alleged.

For example, if a particular offence legally requires a specific element and the FIR does not allege facts capable of establishing that element, the accused may argue that continuing the prosecution would not be justified. Whether this argument succeeds depends on the exact statutory provision and the allegations as a whole.

### 2. The Allegations Do Not Justify the Investigation in Law
A quashing petition may raise a jurisdictional or procedural objection if the allegations do not disclose a cognizable offence and the law does not otherwise permit the police to investigate in the circumstances.

These arguments require careful examination of the applicable procedural provisions. The fact that an offence is described incorrectly or that the accused disagrees with the police does not, without more, establish a ground for quashing.

### 3. The Uncontroverted Material Does Not Disclose an Offence
In a suitable case, the court may examine the FIR and relevant material collected during the investigation to determine whether the allegations, taken in the legally appropriate manner, disclose the offence in question.

This is different from asking the High Court to decide whether every witness is telling the truth or whether the prosecution will ultimately prove guilt. Those evidentiary questions ordinarily belong to the trial process.

### 4. The Proceedings Are Barred by Law
An identifiable legal prohibition may support a challenge. Depending on the case, the issue could concern a mandatory statutory requirement, the competent authority to initiate proceedings, or another express legal bar.

Not every procedural irregularity invalidates a prosecution. The advocate must identify the applicable provision, explain its legal effect and demonstrate why the alleged defect justifies the relief sought.

### 5. The Allegations Are Inherently Improbable
The Bhajan Lal framework recognises cases where the allegations are so inherently absurd or improbable that no prudent person could reasonably conclude that there are sufficient grounds to proceed.

This is not an invitation to conduct a mini-trial. A mere denial by the accused, a dispute about credibility, or a competing version of events will not ordinarily be enough when resolving the disagreement requires detailed evidence.

### 6. The Case Appears to Have Been Instituted Maliciously
A petitioner may argue that criminal proceedings were initiated in bad faith, such as to pursue a private vendetta or misuse the criminal justice system.

However, alleging personal hostility is not enough. The claim should be assessed against the record, the sequence of events, the allegations, and any other material legally relevant to the issue. A prior dispute or an apparent motive does not automatically invalidate an otherwise legally sustainable case.

### 7. Continuing the Proceedings Would Abuse the Court's Process
The High Court's inherent power exists partly to prevent the judicial process from being used for an improper purpose. A case may warrant intervention where its continuation would be legally unjustifiable in the circumstances.

Where a commercial, financial or personal dispute has been presented as a criminal case, the relevant question is not simply whether a civil dispute also exists. The court considers whether the allegations actually disclose a criminal offence and whether the circumstances justify the extraordinary remedy of quashing.

## Can an FIR Be Quashed After a Settlement?

Yes, a genuine settlement can support quashing in an appropriate case, but it does not automatically terminate the FIR.

The Supreme Court explained the distinction between statutory compounding and the High Court's inherent quashing power in **Gian Singh v. State of Punjab (2012)**. The court must consider the nature and gravity of the alleged offence, the public interest involved, the genuineness of the settlement and whether continuing the proceedings would serve the ends of justice.

### Disputes Where a Settlement May Matter
A settlement may be relevant in certain matrimonial or family disputes and in disputes arising predominantly from commercial, financial or other private transactions. The court must still consider whether the allegations constitute serious criminal conduct and whether the prosecution has wider implications beyond the parties.

For an appropriate case, the record may include a written settlement, affidavits or statements confirming that the parties consent voluntarily, and other documents required by the court. The High Court may verify whether consent is genuine and free from coercion.

### When a Settlement Is Unlikely to Be Enough
Serious offences affecting society, including grave violent offences, sexual offences and corruption-related allegations, are not ordinarily quashed merely because the complainant and accused have reached an agreement. The court examines the character of the offence and applicable precedent rather than treating every settlement alike.

There is also an important difference between statutory compounding and quashing. **Section 359 BNSS** governs compounding of specified offences. The High Court's inherent power under Section 528 is distinct and may, in suitable cases, extend to non-compoundable offences. It is not a way to bypass the legal restrictions that apply to serious offences.

## When Is FIR Quashing Unlikely?

A High Court may decline to quash an FIR when the allegations, taken at the appropriate stage and in the legally required manner, disclose an offence and the dispute depends on evidence that should be examined during investigation or trial.

In **Neeharika Infrastructure Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Maharashtra (2021)**, the Supreme Court emphasised that the power to quash is to be exercised cautiously and that courts should not routinely interfere with legitimate investigations. Interim protection against investigation or coercive action is not automatic merely because a quashing petition has been filed.

A petition may face difficulty where:
- The allegations disclose the essential ingredients of a recognised offence.
- The main arguments require disputed witness accounts or other evidence to be tested.
- The petitioner relies only on a bare denial or a general allegation of false implication.
- A settlement is offered for an offence where the public interest or gravity of the alleged crime weighs against quashing.
- The alleged procedural defect does not legally justify terminating the proceedings.

These considerations do not mean that a legally defective case can never be quashed. They explain why the legal ground must be supported by the record and why a quashing petition is not a substitute for a trial.

## FIR Quashing Procedure Step by Step

The procedure varies according to the relevant High Court's rules, the stage of the criminal case and the relief requested. The following is a general outline:

1. **Step 1: Obtain and Review the FIR**  
   Obtain a copy of the FIR and identify the alleged offences, relevant dates, police station and investigating agency. Review the factual allegations rather than relying only on the section numbers recorded by the police.

2. **Step 2: Collect the Relevant Case Records**  
   Gather the complaint and supporting documents available to you, statements or other records lawfully obtained, the chargesheet or final report if filed, and any court orders already passed. Relevant contracts, correspondence, transaction records or settlement documents may also matter.

3. **Step 3: Identify the Specific Legal Grounds**  
   The advocate should map the allegations to the legal ingredients of the offences and identify the grounds on which the High Court is being asked to intervene. Grounds must be based on the facts and relevant law rather than general claims that the case is unjustified.

4. **Step 4: Determine the Correct Court and Procedural Route**  
   The petition is generally filed before the High Court with jurisdiction over the matter. The applicable provision, parties to be included, documents, court fees and filing requirements must be checked under the relevant High Court's rules and the applicable transition provisions.

5. **Step 5: Prepare and File the Petition**  
   The petition usually sets out the facts, identifies the FIR and related proceedings, states the specific grounds for quashing, and explains the relief sought. Supporting documents and the required affidavit or verification are filed according to the applicable rules.

6. **Step 6: Address Notice, Responses and Interim Relief**  
   The High Court may issue notice to the State and other relevant parties, seek responses, call for records or examine the complainant's position. The procedure can differ in settlement-based petitions and in cases involving particular statutes. If protection against arrest or a stay of investigation is needed, the available remedies and the grounds for seeking them must be considered separately.

7. **Step 7: Attend the Hearing and Obtain the Order**  
   The court considers maintainability, the legal grounds, the materials it may properly examine and the objections raised. It may allow the petition, reject it or grant relief limited to particular proceedings or particular accused persons. When an order is made, obtain and review the operative directions carefully.

## Documents Required for an FIR Quashing Petition

The exact filing checklist is jurisdiction-specific, but the following documents are commonly relevant:

- A certified copy of the FIR and the initial complaint or first information.
- The chargesheet, final report or other investigation records, if prepared and available.
- Orders passed by the Magistrate or other courts, including cognizance or summoning orders where applicable.
- Relevant contracts, payment records, correspondence or other documents supporting a specific legal ground.
- A written settlement agreement, proof of voluntary consent and associated affidavits where settlement is relied upon.
- Documents or other admissible material supporting a properly particularised procedural, jurisdictional or statutory objection.
- The required petition, affidavit, vakalatnama / authorization, and annexures specified by the relevant High Court.

*Note: Not every case requires every item. Do not submit confidential, unlawfully obtained or irrelevant material without obtaining legal advice about its use and disclosure.*

### Legal Fees and Court Expenses
There is no single nationwide lawyer fee or total cost that applies to every FIR quashing petition. The amount depends on the advocate, the High Court, the complexity and seriousness of the alleged offences, the volume of records, the number of hearings and whether connected applications or proceedings are involved.

Ask the advocate for a written explanation of professional fees, filing and documentation expenses, and whether further hearings or connected proceedings are charged separately.

### How Long Does It Take?
The time required is case-specific. Relevant factors include the High Court's workload, the need to notify other parties, the stage of the criminal proceedings, the completeness of the record, interim applications and whether the legal issues are contested. An uncontested matter may proceed differently from a complex case involving multiple accused persons.

## FIR Quashing vs. Police Closure Report vs. Compounding

These three legal procedures are related to terminating criminal proceedings, but they operate through different authorities and legal mechanisms:

### A. FIR Quashing
- **Jurisdiction & Authority:** High Court (Section 528 BNSS / Section 482 CrPC).
- **Scope & Meaning:** The High Court exercises inherent judicial power to set aside the FIR, chargesheet, or criminal proceedings when the legal tests for abuse of process or miscarriage of justice are satisfied.

### B. Police Closure Report ('B' / Final Report)
- **Jurisdiction & Authority:** Investigating Agency and the Competent Magistrate.
- **Scope & Meaning:** Upon completing the investigation, if the police find insufficient evidence or conclude the case is unmerited, they file a final report. The Magistrate evaluates the report and decides whether to accept closure or issue notice to the complainant.

### C. Statutory Compounding
- **Jurisdiction & Authority:** Parties & Trial Court (Section 359 BNSS / Section 320 CrPC).
- **Scope & Meaning:** A lawful settlement directly between the complainant and the accused for specified compoundable offences under the Code, resulting in an acquittal by the court.

> **Important Difference:** A police closure report is not the same as a High Court quashing order. Likewise, a private settlement between parties does not automatically quash an FIR—a formal judicial order under Section 528 BNSS / Section 482 CrPC is required.

## Frequently Asked Questions About FIR Quashing

### 1. Can an FIR be quashed after the chargesheet has been filed?
Yes. The High Court may quash a chargesheet or the criminal proceedings arising from an FIR in an appropriate case. Filing a chargesheet does not automatically extinguish the court's inherent powers under Section 528 BNSS / Section 482 CrPC.

### 2. Can an FIR be quashed without the complainant's consent?
Yes. A quashing petition may be considered on independent legal grounds (such as lack of ingredients of an offence, legal bar, or manifest abuse of process) even when there is no settlement. A complainant's consent is not a universal prerequisite to quashing.

### 3. Can the police quash an FIR?
No. Police officers do not exercise judicial inherent powers to quash an FIR. After completing an investigation, the police may submit a closure report or 'B' report where appropriate, which must be accepted by the competent Magistrate.

### 4. Does filing a quashing petition automatically stop the investigation?
No. Filing a petition does not automatically stay an ongoing investigation or protect against arrest. Any interim relief requires a specific judicial order from the High Court upon demonstrating urgency and merit.

### 5. Can a false FIR always be quashed?
No. Simply calling an FIR "false" or claiming innocence is not enough on its own. The petitioner must establish a legally recognized ground under the Bhajan Lal principles. Where the dispute turns on contested facts or witness credibility, courts ordinarily leave those questions for trial.

### 6. Can a non-compoundable offence be quashed on compromise?
In certain cases, yes. The High Court's inherent power under Section 528 BNSS / Section 482 CrPC is distinct from statutory compounding under Section 359 BNSS. However, this power is applied primarily to disputes of a private, commercial, or matrimonial character, and not to grave, heinous, or violent crimes against society.

### 7. Which section should be mentioned in a quashing petition?
Section 528 BNSS is the counterpart to Section 482 CrPC. The applicable provision depends on the date of FIR registration, stage of proceedings, and procedural transition provisions.

### 8. Can an FIR and a chargesheet both be quashed?
Yes. Where the legal grounds justify it, the High Court can grant comprehensive relief quashing both the initial FIR and any consequential proceedings, including the chargesheet and summoning orders.
